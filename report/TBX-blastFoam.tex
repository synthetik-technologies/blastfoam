\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

This report describes progress in using and extending \texttt{blastFoam} to simulate aluminium-enhanced explosive detonation and afterburning in air. The process begins with the propagation of the detonation wave through the aluminium-loaded solid explosive from one or more specified ignition points and continues as the blast wave propagates into the surrounding air, with the detonation products and aluminium mixing with this air and undergoing additional combustion.

The simulation must capture several phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item detonation propagation and early product expansion governed by a high-pressure equation of state
    \item mixing of detonation products with the surrounding air and finite-rate chemical reactions in this mixture
    \item the transport, heating, ignition, and combustion of aluminium particles dispersed within the gas cloud
    \item afterburning of aluminium in the surrounding air and detonation products mixture
\end{enumerate}
The following sections describe the implementation and assessment of physical models, numerical schemes, and solver extensions to simulate these stages within the \texttt{blastFoam} framework.

\texttt{blastFoam} is an open-source compressible CFD solver suite built upon OpenFOAM, developed by Synthetik Applied Technologies \cite{synthetik}. It is designed specifically for high-explosive detonation, shock wave propagation, airblast, and multi-material/multiphase fluid-structure interaction. It implements high-order Godunov-type shock-capturing schemes, advanced thermodynamic equations of state, and multidimensional adaptive mesh refinement (AMR). Relevant solvers in the suite include \texttt{blastFoam} for single- and multiphase detonation and shock propagation; \texttt{blastEulerFoam} for multi-fluid Eulerian-Eulerian exchange problems; \texttt{blastReactingFoam} for finite-rate chemistry with multi-species transport; \texttt{blastParcelFoam} for coupled Eulerian-Lagrangian discrete particle clouds; and \texttt{blastRadFoam} for coupled compressible gas dynamics and thermal radiation transport.

\section{Explosive Charge Composition and Detonation Modelling}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Composition of Aluminized Explosives}

In an aluminized explosive, the initial charge is a heterogeneous mixture of the high-explosive (HE) and solid aluminium particles. These aluminium particles undergo only very limited reaction within the initial detonation wave and rather react with the hot detonation product gases in the expansion wave behind the detonation front and with the surrounding air heated by the blast wave. During the initial stages, the inert aluminium particles act as a momentum (and to a lesser extent, thermal) sink, absorbing kinetic and thermal energy from the detonation wave and reducing the effective CJ pressure relative to an aluminium-free formulation. It is only later that the aluminium reacts with the detonation products and the surrounding air to release additional energy.

\texttt{blastFoam} combines the solid unreacted explosive and the gaseous detonation products in a single `detonating' phase. Within this phase, a reaction progress variable $\lambda$ smoothly transitions the thermodynamic state from the unreacted solid EOS to the fully-reacted product EOS, but individual product species are not explicitly tracked.

The portion of aluminium that remains inert during the initial detonation process is beneficially incorporated into the simulation as a separate solid/granular phase. This allows for the dynamic evolution of the aluminium as a distinct phase in the early evolving flowfield, rather than tying it to the detonation products, improving predictions of the relative phase velocities, thermal energy partitioning and early particle burning.

Note that this treatment of aluminium as a separate phase means that care is needed when determining a detonation product JWL EOS using thermochemical codes. While the code must include the aluminium in the reactant mix, the parameters for the JWL EOS should be determined for the detonation products alone, with the inert portion of the aluminium modelled with its individual EOS.

% ---
\subsection{Programmed Burn}

The propagation of the detonation through the solid explosive is currently modelled in \texttt{blastFoam} using a `Programmed Burn' model:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Burn Fraction Formulation:} The reaction progress variable $\lambda(\mathbf{x}, t)$ is computed based on distance from the designated ignition point $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ign}}$:
          \begin{equation}
              \lambda(\mathbf{x}, t) = \min\left(1, \, \max\left(0, \, \frac{t - t_0}{\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_{\text{ign}}\| / D_{\text{CJ}}}\right)\right)
          \end{equation}
          where $D_{\text{CJ}}$ is the Chapman--Jouguet (CJ) detonation velocity and $t_0$ is the initiation time. This formulation geometrically advances a reaction front outward from the ignition point at the prescribed CJ speed, sweeping the explosive from unreacted to fully reacted state without requiring detailed detonation chemistry.
    \item \textbf{Thermodynamic Blending:} Energy release and the thermodynamic state transition from the unreacted solid EOS to the JWL/JWLC product EOS are performed via linear blending in internal energy:
          \begin{equation}
              e(\rho, T) = (1 - \lambda)\,e_{\text{solid}}(\rho, T) + \lambda\,e_{\text{JWL}}(\rho, T)
          \end{equation}
          The solid phase is typically described by a Murnaghan-type EOS calibrated to match the known initial density and shock Hugoniot. As $\lambda \to 1$ the material transitions fully to the high-pressure detonation product EOS.
    \item \textbf{Input Calibration:} The detonation wave speed $D_{\text{CJ}}$, detonation pressure $p_{\text{CJ}}$, and product equilibrium compositions are obtained from thermochemical equilibrium codes (e.g., CHEETAH or EuDetCode). These tools solve thermodynamic equilibrium at the CJ state accounting for all relevant product species and their dissociation/recombination reactions, providing the EOS constants $(A, B, R_1, R_2, \omega)$ and the reference temperature $T_{\text{CJ}}$.
    \item \textbf{Reactivity of Aluminium within the Detonation Zone:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Micro-scale Al ($>1\,\mu\text{m}$):} Particles remain largely unreacted within the narrow detonation reaction zone ($\sim 10\text{--}100\,\text{ns}$), acting primarily as an inert thermal and momentum sink. Their oxide shell prevents rapid ignition at these timescales.
              \item \emph{Nano-scale Al ($<100\,\text{nm}$):} Can undergo partial fast oxidation within the early Taylor expansion wave behind the CJ state, as the dramatically increased surface-area-to-volume ratio and lower oxide-shell rupture temperatures allow ignition within the detonation reaction timescale.
              \item \emph{Secondary Combustion:} The remaining unreacted aluminium is dispersed outward and participates in secondary afterburning upon turbulent mixing with surrounding atmospheric oxygen. This energy release occurs over milliseconds to tens of milliseconds and is responsible for the characteristic blast enhancement and extended positive impulse phase of aluminized explosives.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Equations of State for Detonation Products}

The detonation products immediately behind the detonation front are modelled using the Jones--Wilkins--Lee (JWL) family of equations of state.

\subsubsection{JWLC Formulation}

The \emph{JWLC} EOS extends the standard JWL formulation with an additional thermal term $\omega C_v T$ for improved high-pressure, high-density calibration:
\begin{equation}
    p = A\left(1 - \frac{\omega \rho}{R_1 \rho_0}\right)e^{-R_1 \rho_0 / \rho} + B\left(1 - \frac{\omega \rho}{R_2 \rho_0}\right)e^{-R_2 \rho_0 / \rho} + \omega \rho e
\end{equation}
where $A, B, R_1, R_2, \omega$ are empirical fitting constants and $\rho_0$ is the initial explosive density. The two exponential terms capture the repulsive short-range interactions of the detonation products at high compression, while the final thermal term $\omega \rho e$ approximates the Gr\"uneisen pressure contribution.

\subsubsection{eJWLC Formulation and Cold-Curve Isentropic Energy}

A new energy-based formulation, \emph{eJWLC}, has been implemented in \texttt{blastFoam} to provide a thermodynamically consistent Mie--Gr\"uneisen formulation built upon the full three-term ($C$-form) isentrope reference curve.

In this formulation, the isentropic reference pressure $p_s(V)$ along the principal Chapman--Jouguet isentrope ($s = s_{\text{CJ}}$) is defined as a function of the relative volume $V = \rho_0 / \rho = v / v_0$ (where $v_0 = 1/\rho_0$ is the initial specific volume):
\begin{equation}
    p_s(V) = A \exp(-R_1 V) + B \exp(-R_2 V) + C V^{-(\omega+1)}
\end{equation}
The terms $A \exp(-R_1 V)$ and $B \exp(-R_2 V)$ dominate the high-density and intermediate expansion regimes respectively, while the third term $C V^{-(\omega+1)}$ captures the power-law isentrope at large expansion volumes, significantly improving accuracy over standard JWL.

The \emph{cold-curve isentropic energy} $e_s(V)$ represents the internal energy of the expanding detonation products along this reference isentrope. Applying the first law of thermodynamics under isentropic conditions ($\mathrm{d}s = 0$):
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{d}e_s = -p_s\,\mathrm{d}v = -v_0 p_s(V)\,\mathrm{d}V
\end{equation}
Integrating this differential relation analytically with respect to $V$ yields the closed-form expression for the isentropic energy:
\begin{align}
    e_s(V) & = -v_0 \int p_s(V)\,\mathrm{d}V \nonumber                                                                         \\
           & = v_0 \left[ \frac{A}{R_1} \exp(-R_1 V) + \frac{B}{R_2} \exp(-R_2 V) + \frac{C}{\omega} V^{-\omega} \right] + E_0
\end{align}
where $E_0$ is the integration constant corresponding to the reference internal energy datum (the detonation energy per unit mass at infinite expansion).

The complete Mie--Gr\"uneisen pressure in \texttt{eJWLC} is then evaluated as the isentropic base pressure plus a thermal correction proportional to the excess energy above the isentrope:
\begin{equation}
    p(\rho, e) = p_s(V) + \omega \rho \left(e - e_s(V)\right)
\end{equation}
where $\omega$ serves as the constant Gr\"uneisen coefficient $\Gamma = v (\partial p / \partial e)_v = \omega$.

To provide a consistent temperature field, the reference isentrope temperature is given analytically by:
\begin{equation}
    T_s(V) = T_{\text{CJ}} \left(\frac{V_{\text{CJ}}}{V}\right)^{\omega_T}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\text{CJ}} = \rho_0 / \rho_{\text{CJ}}$ and $T_{\text{CJ}}$ is the Chapman--Jouguet temperature from \texttt{EuDetCode}. The temperature exponent $\omega_T$ (\texttt{omegaT}) defaults to $\omega$. Because the pressure depends only on $e - e_s(V)$, $\omega_T$ affects the energy--temperature relation but not the pressure, and it can be chosen so that $T_s(V)$ also passes through a known temperature at a second state (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_conservation}):
\begin{equation}
    \omega_T = \frac{\ln\left(T_{\text{CJ}}/T_{\text{ref}}\right)}{\ln\left(V_{\text{ref}}/V_{\text{CJ}}\right)}
\end{equation}
A value $\omega_T \neq \omega$ is not strictly thermodynamically consistent with a constant-$C_v$ Mie--Gr\"uneisen form; it is used as a temperature closure only. When paired with a caloric model (\texttt{eConst}) having specific heat $C_v$, the local thermodynamic temperature recovers correctly both on and off the isentrope:
\begin{equation}
    T(\rho, e) = T_s(V) + \frac{e - e_s(V)}{C_v}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Parameter Determination}

The parameters $(A, B, C, R_1, R_2, \omega, E_0, C_v)$ are determined from thermochemical equilibrium codes (such as CHEETAH or EuDetCode):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Equilibrium Isentrope Calculation:} The thermochemical code solves chemical equilibrium at the Chapman--Jouguet state to obtain $(\rho_{\text{CJ}}, p_{\text{CJ}}, T_{\text{CJ}}, e_{\text{CJ}}, D_{\text{CJ}})$, and then computes the expansion path along the isentrope ($s = s_{\text{CJ}}$) down to large volume expansions ($V/V_{\text{CJ}} \approx 10\text{--}20$).
    \item \textbf{Non-linear Regression:} The coefficients $(A, B, C, R_1, R_2, \omega)$ and energy datum $E_0$ are fitted simultaneously via non-linear least-squares to reproduce the computed expansion curve $(p_s, e_s)$ over the entire density range from $\rho_0$ down to $\rho_0/20$.
    \item \textbf{Aluminized Explosive Calibration:} As noted in Section~2.1, for aluminized charges the thermochemical calculation includes the full reactant stoichiometry, but the JWL EOS parameters are calibrated specifically for the gaseous detonation products, with the inert aluminium volume excluded to maintain correct gas-phase thermodynamics.
\end{enumerate}

An example calibration for an aluminized formulation comprising 70\% Composition~B and 30\% aluminium by mass, where 90\% of the aluminium is constrained as chemically inert within the detonation zone (corresponding to 27\% inert Al by total charge mass), is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:isentrope_comparison}, with the corresponding fitted parameters summarized in Table~\ref{tab:jwl_parameters}. The expansion isentropes computed with EuDetCode are shown for the nearly incompressible inert aluminium ($v_{\text{inert}}$), the expanding reactive detonation product gases ($v_{\text{prod}}$), and the homogenized full mixture ($v_{\text{total}}$), alongside their corresponding JWL analytical fits. When the solid aluminium is tracked dynamically as an independent phase in \texttt{blastFoam}, the JWL equation of state is fitted to the reactive product isentrope ($v_{\text{prod}}$), ensuring that the gas compressibility, wave speeds, and expansion thermodynamics are accurately captured without double-counting the volume occupied by the inert aluminium particles.

\subsubsection{Notes}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-Pressure Regime Limitations:} While JWLC and \texttt{eJWLC} are accurate in the high-pressure detonation and early expansion regimes ($p > 1\,\text{GPa}$), their physical validity deteriorates during extreme expansion ($V/V_0 > 10\text{--}20$). This limitation motivates the phase transfer mechanism described in Section~3, which transfers material to a Noble-Abel EOS once expansion conditions are met.
    \item \textbf{Difficulties in Temperature Determination:} The original JWL and JWLC formulations are purely mechanical (caloric) equations relating $p$, $v$, and $e$. They lack a fundamental thermodynamic potential and do not explicitly define $T$ or entropy $s$, making the thermodynamic state under-determined without an additional caloric closure. While the \texttt{eJWLC} model (Section~2.3.2) introduces an explicit reference temperature $T_s(V)$ and caloric closure $C_v$, temperature determination in expanded detonation products remains subject to several physical approximations:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Reference Isentrope Temperature:} The power-law relation $T_s(V) = T_{\text{CJ}}(V_{\text{CJ}}/V)^\omega$ assumes a constant Gr\"uneisen coefficient $\omega$. As demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:temperature_comparison_products}, which compares the true thermochemical equilibrium isentrope temperature computed by EuDetCode ($T_{\text{true}}$) against the \texttt{eJWLC} model ($T_{\text{JWL}}$) for the 70\% Comp-B / 30\% Al formulation, shifting chemical equilibria and endothermic dissociation during expansion cause the true gas temperature to drop much more rapidly than predicted by the power law, resulting in overpredictions of up to $500\text{--}800\,\text{K}$ across intermediate expansion pressures ($0.01\text{--}1\,\text{GPa}$).
              \item \emph{Constant $C_v$ Assumption:} Off-isentrope temperature evaluation via $T = T_s(V) + (e - e_s(V))/C_v$ with a constant $C_v$ fails to capture the effects of shifting chemical equilibria (molecular dissociation and recombination) and vibrational excitation across the temperature range $500\text{--}4000\,\text{K}$.
              \item \emph{Variable Gr\"uneisen Parameter:} The constant coefficient $\omega \approx 0.25\text{--}0.35$ is calibrated to fit high-density detonation states. As the gas expands toward ideal-gas conditions ($\Gamma = \gamma - 1$), the JWL temperature increasingly diverges from the true thermodynamic temperature.
          \end{itemize}

          \begin{figure}[htbp]
              \centering
              \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/temperature_comparison_products.png}
              \caption{Comparison of temperature along the Chapman--Jouguet expansion isentrope for the reactive detonation products of 70\% Comp-B / 30\% Al (with 90\% inert Al), comparing the full thermochemical equilibrium calculation from EuDetCode ($T_{\text{true}}$) with the \texttt{eJWLC} isentrope model ($T_{\text{JWL}}$) as a function of pressure (left) and specific volume (right).}
              \label{fig:temperature_comparison_products}
          \end{figure}

    \item \textbf{Impact on Al Ignition:} Errors of several hundred Kelvin in the JWL temperature field can affect the accuracy of thermal transfer to the aluminium particles, and hence the ignition delay and afterburning rates.
\end{itemize}

\section{Detonation Product Phase Transfer}
% ============================================================

The `detonating' phase represents a homogenized mixture in which individual species concentrations are not explicitly transported. Once the detonation products have expanded sufficiently, they must be transferred into a fully reactive multicomponent gas phase that can track species composition and finite-rate chemistry. This section describes both the transfer mechanism and the thermodynamic framework of the receiving phase.

% ---
\subsection{Phase Transfer Mechanism}

\subsubsection{Rationale for Pressure-Interval Rather Than Temperature-Driven Transfer}
In early blast modeling approaches, phase change from condensed or detonating products to gas was often parameterized using temperature thresholds (e.g., triggering transfer when $T < T_{\text{threshold}}$). However, rigorous thermodynamic and numerical investigation revealed that temperature-driven triggering is fundamentally flawed when applied to JWL-based formulations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inaccuracy of JWL Caloric Temperatures:} The JWL and \texttt{eJWLC} equations of state are mechanical models empirically calibrated against hydrodynamic work output $p\,\mathrm{d}V$ measured in copper cylinder expansion tests and thermochemical code isentropes. The internal temperature in JWL is typically evaluated via a constant Gr\"uneisen exponent $\omega$:
          \begin{equation}
              T(V) = T_{\text{CJ}} \left(\frac{V_{\text{CJ}}}{V}\right)^\omega
          \end{equation}
          or by dividing sensible internal energy by an artificial, constant specific heat $c_v$. Because $\omega$ is treated as constant (typically $\omega \approx 0.25\text{--}0.38$) rather than reflecting the real-gas, composition-dependent thermodynamic state, JWL severely mispredicts detonation product temperatures during expansion. As demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pv_curve_comparison}(bottom-left) and Section~\ref{sec:thermo_reconciliation}, the JWL temperature along the expansion path diverges by hundreds of Kelvin from the true thermochemical equilibrium temperature calculated by \texttt{EuDetCode}.
    \item \textbf{Pathological Triggering in Fireball Regimes:} Because the JWL temperature drops at an unphysical rate during rapid expansion and exhibits strong sensitivity to artificial Gr\"uneisen parameters, a temperature-based trigger activates unpredictably across the domain, either prematurely initiating transfer in highly expanded core regions or stalling transfer in boundary layers where artificial numerical heating occurs.
    \item \textbf{High Accuracy of the Mechanical Pressure Expansion:} In sharp contrast to temperature, the mechanical expansion pressure $p(V)$ is modeled with exceptional accuracy by \texttt{eJWLC} across several orders of magnitude (from CJ detonation pressures $p_{\text{CJ}} \approx 30\,\text{GPa}$ down to ambient levels $p \approx 1\,\text{bar}$), matching thermochemical codes and experimental cylinder wall velocities within $<2\%$.
    \item \textbf{Physical Alignment with the Freeze-Out Interval:} Detonation product chemistry is known to freeze out over a well-defined pressure window ($p \approx 50\text{--}150\,\text{bar}$), where three-body recombination reactions quench. Selecting a pressure interval $[p_{\text{lower}}, p_{\text{upper}}]$ guarantees that the phase transfer activates precisely within this physically grounded freeze-out window regardless of local caloric temperature discrepancies.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Mathematical Formulation of the \texttt{pressurePhaseChange} Model}
To implement this physical framework, the \texttt{pressurePhaseChange} \texttt{fvModel} was developed for OpenFOAM-12 (under \path{src/finiteVolume/fvModels/pressurePhaseChange/}, superseding the temperature-based model).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pressure Activation Factor $\Gamma(p)$:}
          The transfer rate is modulated by a smooth, continuously differentiable two-sided activation factor:
          \begin{equation}
              \begin{split}
                  \Gamma(p) = \frac{1}{4} & \left(1 + \tanh\left(\frac{p_{\text{upper}} - p}{p_{\text{range}}}\right)\right) \left(1 + \tanh\left(\frac{p - p_{\text{lower}}}{p_{\text{range}}}\right)\right) \\
                  & \times \mathcal{H}\left(p_{\text{upper}} + 2p_{\text{range}} - p\right) \cdot \mathcal{H}\left(p - (p_{\text{lower}} - 2p_{\text{range}})\right)
              \end{split}
          \end{equation}
          where $p_{\text{lower}}$ and $p_{\text{upper}}$ define the user-selected transfer interval (configured via \texttt{pInterval (pMin pMax)} or \texttt{pLower}/\texttt{pUpper}), $p_{\text{range}}$ is the smoothing transition width (typically $0.05\text{--}0.10 \times (p_{\text{upper}} - p_{\text{lower}})$), and $\mathcal{H}$ is the Heaviside step function ensuring that activation vanishes identically outside the transition envelope. If $p_{\text{lower}} = 0$, the factor smoothly reduces to a one-sided low-pressure trigger:
          \begin{equation}
              \Gamma(p) = \frac{1}{2} \left(1 + \tanh\left(\frac{p_{\text{upper}} - p}{p_{\text{range}}}\right)\right) \cdot \mathcal{H}\left(p_{\text{upper}} + 2p_{\text{range}} - p\right)
          \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Phase Mass Transfer Rate:}
          The volumetric mass transfer rate from source phase to target phase is governed by:
          \begin{equation}
              \dot{m}_{\text{transfer}} = \frac{\alpha_{\text{src}} \rho_{\text{src}}}{\tau} \cdot \Gamma(p)
          \end{equation}
          where $\tau$ is the characteristic relaxation timescale (chosen small relative to convective transport timescales, e.g., $\tau \sim 10^{-5}\text{--}10^{-4}\,\text{s}$, but sufficiently large to preserve numerical stability and prevent acoustic shocks).
    \item \textbf{Multi-Species Allocation:}
          Product mass transferred to the receiving gas phase is mapped across individual chemical species according to prescribed frozen equilibrium mass fractions $Y_k$:
          \begin{equation}
              \dot{m}_{k} = Y_k^{\text{target}} \, \dot{m}_{\text{transfer}}, \quad \sum_k Y_k^{\text{target}} = 1.0
          \end{equation}
          supporting species allocation via either mass fractions or mole fractions ($X_k$, converted to mass fractions with the species molecular weights, $Y_k = X_k W_k / \sum_j X_j W_j$).
    \item \textbf{Inter-Phase Energy Conservation:}
          The mixture total energy is a conserved variable, so moving mass between phases without an energy source conserves energy exactly, provided all phases measure energy from a common datum. The datums of the source EOS (\texttt{eJWLC}) and the target mixture (JANAF / Noble--Abel) are reconciled through the products reference energy $E_0$ (Section~\ref{sec:thermo_reconciliation}). Optionally, a constant datum shift $e_{\text{offset}}$ (\texttt{eOffset}, default 0) can be added to the transferred mass, $S_e = \dot{m}_{\text{transfer}}\, e_{\text{offset}}$, for cases that keep the source EOS on a different datum. Because it is constant, this is bookkeeping: the datum-corrected total energy remains conserved. A state-dependent offset is deliberately not used, since it would add or remove real energy (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_conservation}).
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Reactive Gas Phase Modelling}

Once detonation products have been transferred, they are evolved within a multicomponent reactive gas phase governed by the Noble-Abel equation of state.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multiphase Multicomponent Thermodynamics:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \texttt{multicomponentPhaseFluidBlastThermo}: Enables individual phases to carry distinct multi-species compositions ($Y_k$) with individual transport and mixture properties. Each species $k$ has its own molecular weight $W_k$, specific heat polynomial $c_{p,k}(T)$, and reference enthalpy $h_{f,k}^0$, allowing accurate tracking of composition-dependent thermodynamic properties as products mix with air and react.
              \item The \texttt{reactingPhaseFluidBlastThermo} class couples multicomponent thermodynamics with \texttt{blastCombustionModel} for per-phase finite-rate chemistry, exporting the heat release rate $\dot{Q}_{\text{chem}}$ ($[\text{W/m}^3]$) as a volumetric source term in the energy equation.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Species Transport and Kinetics:} Detonation product gases ($\text{CO}, \text{CO}_2, \text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{H}_2, \text{N}_2, \text{C}_{(\text{s})}, \text{Al}_{(\text{g})}$) and atmospheric air are evolved using finite-rate chemical reaction mechanisms. Each species mass fraction $Y_k$ is governed by:
          \begin{equation}
              \frac{\partial (\rho Y_k)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u} Y_k) = \nabla \cdot (\rho D_k \nabla Y_k) + \dot{\omega}_k
          \end{equation}
          where $D_k$ is the species diffusivity and $\dot{\omega}_k = W_k \sum_r \nu_{k,r} \mathcal{R}_r$ is the net production rate from all $r$ elementary reactions. In post-detonation fireball regimes, simplified mechanisms (e.g., CO oxidation, $\text{H}_2$ combustion) are generally sufficient as the product mixture is dominated by stable species near equilibrium.
    \item \textbf{Noble-Abel (Co-volume) Equation of State:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Equation of State:} Accounts for the finite non-zero volume occupied by gas molecules at elevated pressures:
                    \begin{equation}
                        p\left(\frac{1}{\rho} - b\right) = R_{\text{mix}} T \implies p = \frac{\rho R_{\text{mix}} T}{1 - b \rho}
                    \end{equation}
                    where $b = \sum_k Y_k b_k$ is the mixture co-volume ($\text{m}^3/\text{kg}$) and $R_{\text{mix}} = \mathcal{R}_u / W_{\text{mix}}$ is the mixture gas constant. The co-volume $b \approx 10^{-4}\text{--}10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3/\text{kg}$ physically represents the excluded volume per unit mass of dense detonation product gases.
              \item \emph{Speed of Sound:} The co-volume compressibility correction increases the effective sound speed relative to the ideal gas at the same $(\rho, T)$ state:
                    \begin{equation}
                        c = \frac{\sqrt{\gamma R_{\text{mix}} T}}{1 - b \rho}
                    \end{equation}
                    while temperature-dependent specific heats $c_p(T), c_v(T)$ are evaluated using NASA 7-coefficient polynomials with temperature clamping.
              \item \emph{Physical Range:} The Noble-Abel model accurately describes the intermediate pressure regime ($10\text{--}500\,\text{bar}$) where molecular volume effects remain significant, and smoothly asymptotes to the ideal gas law ($b\rho \ll 1$) in the far field. This range corresponds to the first few hundred microseconds after detonation, when pressures have decayed from the CJ state ($\sim 20\text{--}30\,\text{GPa}$) but have not yet reached ambient conditions.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Thermodynamic Consistency and Reference Energy Reconciliation}
\label{sec:thermo_reconciliation}

A critical challenge in coupling a condensed-phase detonation model (\texttt{eJWLC}) with a multi-species reacting gas phase (Noble-Abel with JANAF thermochemistry) is ensuring physical and thermodynamic consistency across the phase transfer interface. Although finite-volume solvers enforce strict conservation of mass, momentum, and total energy, transferring fluid across distinct equations of state can induce artificial pressure spikes, temperature errors, and energy offsets unless the thermodynamic reference states are rigorously reconciled.

\subsubsection{The Thermodynamic Datum Discrepancy}
The fundamental source of mismatch between the two descriptions arises from their differing energy reference datums:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thermochemical JANAF Datum (\texttt{thermo janaf}):} Standard multi-species thermodynamics defines the specific enthalpy $h_k(T)$ and internal energy $e_k(T)$ relative to standard elemental reference states at $T_{\text{ref}} = 298.15\,\text{K}$ and $p_{\text{ref}} = 1\,\text{bar}$, incorporating the standard enthalpy of formation $\Delta H_{f,k}^\circ$:
          \begin{equation}
              h_k(T) = \Delta H_{f,k}^\circ(T_{\text{ref}}) + \int_{T_{\text{ref}}}^T c_{p,k}(T')\,\mathrm{d}T', \quad e_k(T) = h_k(T) - R_k T
          \end{equation}
          Because stable detonation products ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) possess large negative heats of formation, the baseline energy of the product gas mixture is strongly negative ($e_{\text{gas}}(T_{\text{ref}}) \approx -5.73\,\text{MJ/kg}$ for 70\% Comp-B / 30\% Al products).
    \item \textbf{Mechanical JWL Datum (\texttt{thermo eConst}):} The \texttt{eJWLC} equation of state is a mechanical formulation derived for hydrocodes to model the $p(V)$ work capacity along an expansion isentrope:
          \begin{equation}
              e_s(V) = v_0 \left[ \frac{A}{R_1} \mathrm{e}^{-R_1 V} + \frac{B}{R_2} \mathrm{e}^{-R_2 V} + \frac{C}{\omega} V^{-\omega} \right] + E_0
          \end{equation}
          JWL has no intrinsic awareness of molecular identity, chemical bonds, or standard elemental formation states. Its reference energy $E_0$ (or $e_0$) is arbitrary and purely mechanical.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Mass and Energy Matching vs. Thermal Pressure Discontinuity}
When a computational cell transitions from \texttt{compBAl} to \texttt{gas} at the phase transfer threshold ($p_{\text{trans}} \approx 100\,\text{bar}$), the compressible solver strictly conserves mass and internal energy:
\begin{equation}
    v_{\text{trans}} = \frac{1}{\rho_{\text{trans}}} = v_{\text{JWL}}(p_{\text{trans}}), \quad e_{\text{sensible, gas}}(T_{\text{gas}}) = \int_{T_{\text{ref}}}^{T_{\text{gas}}} c_{v,\text{mix}}(T')\,\mathrm{d}T' = e_{\text{JWL}}(v_{\text{trans}})
\end{equation}
Inverting the caloric function $e_{\text{sensible, gas}}(T)$ yields the post-transfer temperature $T_{\text{gas}}$. If the unadjusted JWL reference energy is used ($e_{\text{JWL}} \approx 1.17\,\text{MJ/kg}$ at $100\,\text{bar}$), the inverted gas temperature is $T_{\text{gas}} \approx 1377\,\text{K}$.

Plugging $(v_{\text{trans}}, T_{\text{gas}})$ into the Noble-Abel EOS ($p = \frac{R_{\text{mix}} T}{v - b_{\text{mix}}}$) results in an unphysical pressure jump:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{NA}} \approx 166.8\,\text{bar} \quad \text{vs.} \quad p_{\text{JWL}} = 100.0\,\text{bar} \quad (+66.8\% \text{ artificial pressure jump})
\end{equation}
This jump occurs because the thermal pressure in any EOS scales with the Gr\"uneisen parameter $\Gamma = \frac{1}{\rho}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial e}\right)_v$:
\begin{equation}
    p_{\text{thermal, JWL}} \approx \omega_{\text{JWL}} \, \rho \, e_{\text{JWL}}, \quad p_{\text{thermal, NA}} \approx \frac{\gamma_{\text{mix}} - 1}{1 - b_{\text{mix}}\rho} \, \rho \, e_{\text{thermal}}
\end{equation}
In the Noble-Abel EOS, pressure couples to absolute thermal energy measured from $0\,\text{K}$ ($e_{\text{thermal}} \approx \bar{c}_v T$), whereas the empirical JWL model references energy to an arbitrary datum with a smaller Gr\"uneisen exponent ($\omega_{\text{JWL}} = 0.2387$).

\subsubsection{General Robust Matching Formulation}
To eliminate artificial pressure discontinuities while preserving strict mass and energy conservation, a robust, general four-step thermodynamic matching procedure is established:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Anchor at the Freeze-Out Pressure:} Trigger phase transfer within the physical freeze-out window ($p_{\text{trans}} \approx 100\text{--}200\,\text{bar}$), where chemical reaction kinetics in expanded products slow down relative to hydrodynamic expansion timescales, justifying the constant-species Noble-Abel assumption.
    \item \textbf{Enforce Pressure Continuity ($\Delta p = 0$):} Invert the Noble-Abel EOS at $(v_{\text{trans}}, p_{\text{trans}})$ to determine the exact continuous transition temperature $T_{\text{trans}}$:
          \begin{equation}
              T_{\text{trans}} = \frac{p_{\text{trans}}(v_{\text{trans}} - b_{\text{mix}})}{R_{\text{mix}}}
          \end{equation}
          For the 70\% Comp-B / 30\% Al formulation at $p_{\text{trans}} = 100\,\text{bar}$ ($v_{\text{trans}} = 2.622 \times 10^{-2}\,\text{m}^3/\text{kg}$, $b_{\text{mix}} = 1.263 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3/\text{kg}$, $R_{\text{mix}} = 302.24\,\text{J/(kg\,K)}$), this yields $T_{\text{trans}} = 825.85\,\text{K}$.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Form Reference Energy Offset:} Calculate the products reference energy $E_0$ (defined in \texttt{physicalProperties}) so that the sensible internal energy of the gas matches the JWL internal energy at $T_{\text{trans}}$. This is an offline calibration of a constant datum, so it keeps the transfer energy-conservative; it is not equivalent to a run-time, state-dependent energy source:
          \begin{equation}
              e_{\text{sensible, gas}}(T_{\text{trans}}) = \sum_k Y_k \int_{298.15\,\text{K}}^{T_{\text{trans}}} c_{v,k}(T')\,\mathrm{d}T' = 0.5148\,\text{MJ/kg}
          \end{equation}
          \begin{equation}
              E_0 = e_{\text{sensible, gas}}(T_{\text{trans}}) - v_0 \left[ \frac{A}{R_1} \mathrm{e}^{-R_1 V_{\text{trans}}} + \frac{B}{R_2} \mathrm{e}^{-R_2 V_{\text{trans}}} + \frac{C}{\omega} V_{\text{trans}}^{-\omega} \right] = -583.64\,\text{kJ/kg}
          \end{equation}
          Because $\frac{\mathrm{d}E_0}{\mathrm{d}v} = 0$, adjusting $E_0$ does not alter the eJWLC $p(V)$ isentrope, Chapman--Jouguet pressure, or expansion velocity in any way.
    \item \textbf{Eliminating Physical Tail Error ($C^1$ Continuity):} The remaining difference between the eJWLC mechanical work integral ($\sim 1.098\,\text{MJ/kg}$) and the true caloric gas expansion energy ($\sim 0.515\,\text{MJ/kg}$) represents $\approx 7\text{--}9\%$ of the explosive's total energy, stemming from the constant $\omega$ assumption in the JWL tail. To achieve $C^1$ continuity (matching both $p$ and $\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}v}$ / sound speed $c$), the asymptotic eJWLC parameters should be calibrated directly against the frozen product gas:
          \begin{equation}
              \omega_{\text{JWL}} = \gamma_{\text{mix}}(T_{\text{trans}}) - 1, \quad C = p_{\text{trans}} \cdot V_{\text{trans}}^{\omega_{\text{JWL}} + 1}
          \end{equation}
          This constraint eliminates the tail energy error entirely and prevents double-counting of late-time afterburning energy when explicit combustion models are activated.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Energy Conservation and Temperature Consistency During Transfer}
\label{sec:transfer_conservation}
Pressure continuity at the transfer is the primary requirement; the JWL temperature is known to be inaccurate in the expanded state. Three consequences follow for the \texttt{pressurePhaseChange} model:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No state-dependent energy source.} With a common datum the isochoric transfer at constant specific energy conserves energy exactly. An offset evaluated from the local state, for example $e_{\text{offset}} = e_{\text{gas}}(T) - e_{\text{JWL}}(T)$ at the shared cell temperature, is not a datum shift: it injects energy in proportion to the JWL temperature error. In the 0D reactor with the power-law isentrope temperature ($T = 1318.69\,\text{K}$ at $100\,\text{bar}$), such an offset added $\approx 480\,\text{kJ/kg}$ and raised the pressure to $156.5\,\text{bar}$ ($+56\%$), reproducing the artificial jump the matching procedure is designed to remove.
    \item \textbf{Pressure continuity from the $E_0$ calibration.} With $E_0$ chosen so that $e_{\text{gas}}(v_{\text{trans}}, p_{\text{trans}}) = e_{\text{JWL}}(v_{\text{trans}}, p_{\text{trans}})$, the transfer preserves pressure exactly at the calibration state. Across a finite transfer window ($p \in [p_{\text{lower}}, p_{\text{upper}}]$) the residual mismatch depends on how closely the two equations of state agree near the transfer state, which is the purpose of the tail calibration (step 4). Exact pressure continuity everywhere and exact energy conservation are only simultaneously possible where the two equations of state coincide along the transfer path.
    \item \textbf{Temperature consistency.} If the JWL temperature at the transfer state differs from the gas-EOS temperature, the gas formed by the transfer is at the wrong temperature. In the single-energy \texttt{blastFoam} multiphase system, where the phases share one temperature, energy is then redistributed between the phases as the shared temperature relaxes, producing a transient pressure excursion proportional to $C_v \Delta T$. In \texttt{blastEulerFoam} each phase carries its own energy and temperature, so this redistribution does not occur, but the temperature mismatch still drives the interphase heat transfer. Keeping $T_{\text{CJ}}$ at the \texttt{EuDetCode} value and choosing $\omega_T$ so that $T_s(V_{\text{trans}})$ equals the gas-EOS temperature removes the mismatch without affecting the pressure.
    \item \textbf{Momentum and energy carried with the mass.} In \texttt{blastEulerFoam} the transferred mass $\dot m$ leaves the source phase with its velocity $\mathbf{u}_s$ and specific total energy $E_s$, and each target phase $k$ receives $f_k \dot m\,(1, \mathbf{u}_s, E_s + e_{\text{offset}})$, with the constant datum shift $e_{\text{offset}}$ (default 0). Mass, momentum and datum-corrected total energy are then conserved by the transfer itself.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Turbulent Mixing and Afterburning}
% ============================================================

The effectiveness of aluminium afterburning and detonation product combustion is largely controlled by how efficiently these mix with surrounding oxygen-rich air. This mixing is driven by hydrodynamic instabilities at the product--air interface and is strongly coupled to the turbulent combustion chemistry.

% ---
\subsection{Hydrodynamic Mixing Instabilities}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Richtmyer--Meshkov Instability (RMI):} Triggered when the primary blast wave and subsequent reflected shock waves traverse the density interface (contact discontinuity) separating dense detonation products from ambient air. The impulsive baroclinic vorticity deposition ($\nabla \rho \times \nabla p \neq 0$ across a misaligned density-pressure gradient pair) seeds perturbations that grow as mushroom-cap vortex rings, promoting turbulent mixing at the product--air interface.
    \item \emph{Rayleigh--Taylor Instability (RTI):} Arises during the late deceleration phase as light ambient air decelerates the expanding high-density product core. The characteristic RTI growth rate is $\sigma \propto \sqrt{A_t \, g_{\text{eff}} \, k}$, where $A_t = (\rho_h - \rho_l)/(\rho_h + \rho_l)$ is the Atwood number, $g_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective interfacial deceleration, and $k$ is the perturbation wavenumber.
    \item \emph{Kelvin--Helmholtz Instability (KHI):} Driven by intense tangential velocity shear along the contact discontinuity, particularly along the stalks and slip interfaces of RMI/RTI mushroom plumes as hot detonation products expand into surrounding air. The velocity shear $\Delta U$ destabilizes the interface, rolling up into discrete Spanwise vortices that rapidly break down into fine-scale three-dimensional turbulence. KHI is the critical mechanism driving the turbulent cascade from large-scale coherent structures down to the Batchelor and Kolmogorov scales, dramatically multiplying the interfacial contact area and molecular mixing rate between oxidizer and combustible products.
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Gaseous Products vs.\ Dispersed Aluminium Particles: Mixing Dynamics}
\label{sec:mixing_comparison}

While both the gaseous detonation products ($\text{CO}, \text{H}_2, \text{CH}_4, \dots$) and the dispersed solid/molten aluminium particles ($\text{Al}$) are transported outward by the expanding detonation core and undergo afterburning upon contact with atmospheric $\text{O}_2$, their transport, dispersion, and mixing mechanisms exhibit fundamental physical similarities and critical differences.

\subsubsection{Similarities in Macro-Scale Mixing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common Hydrodynamic Drivers:} Both phases are initially accelerated by the high-pressure Chapman--Jouguet expansion and subsequently subjected to the same macro-scale turbulent flow field generated by Richtmyer--Meshkov and Rayleigh--Taylor instabilities across the expanding fireball boundary.
    \item \textbf{Turbulent Entrainment Dependency:} Neither phase can react with atmospheric oxygen without large-scale turbulent engulfment that draws ambient oxidizer across the contact discontinuity into the fuel-rich product core.
    \item \textbf{Mixing-Controlled Afterburning Regime:} At elevated post-detonation temperatures ($T > 1500\text{--}2000\,\text{K}$), chemical kinetic rates for both gas-phase oxidation and small-particle aluminium oxidation are extremely fast relative to hydrodynamic transport. Consequently, afterburning in both phases is strongly mixing-controlled ($\text{Da} \gg 1$), where the overall rate of energy release is governed by the rate of turbulent mass exchange rather than chemical kinetics.
    \item \textbf{Exothermic Thermal Feedback:} Combustion of both gaseous products and aluminium releases massive volumetric heat ($\sim 31\,\text{MJ/kg}$ for Al, $\sim 10\,\text{MJ/kg}$ for CO), which dramatically increases local gas temperatures, lowers fluid density, accelerates fireball expansion, and enhances baroclinic vorticity generation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Fundamental Differences in Transport and Micro-Scale Mixing}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inertial Decoupling and the Stokes Number ($St$):}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Gaseous Products ($St = 0$):} Gaseous molecules have zero particle inertia and behave as a continuum. They follow fluid streamlines instantaneously, diffusing at the molecular level through turbulent eddies down to the Batchelor scale.
              \item \emph{Aluminium Particles ($St \neq 0$):} The particulate phase possesses finite inertia characterized by the particle Stokes number:
                    \begin{equation}
                        St = \frac{\tau_p}{\tau_f} = \frac{\rho_p d_p^2 / (18 \mu_g)}{\tau_f}
                    \end{equation}
                    where $\tau_p$ is the particle momentum relaxation time and $\tau_f$ is the characteristic fluid eddy timescale. The mixing behavior is highly size-dependent:
                    \begin{itemize}
                        \item \emph{Ballistic Regime ($St > 1$, e.g., $d_p > 15\text{--}30\,\mu\text{m}$):} Particles decouple from the decelerating gas flow at the contact surface and penetrate ballistically into virgin, oxygen-rich ambient air ahead of the gaseous fireball.
                        \item \emph{Tracer Regime ($St \ll 1$, e.g., sub-micron nano-Al):} Particles remain dynamically locked to the carrier gas, advecting with turbulent eddies and mixing identically to the gaseous species.
                        \item \emph{Preferential Concentration ($St \sim 1$):} Particles are centrifuged out of high-vorticity eddy cores and accumulate in low-vorticity, high-strain braid regions between vortices, creating intense local inhomogeneities in particle concentration and local equivalence ratios.
                    \end{itemize}
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Micro-Scale Mixing and Flame Morphology:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Gas-Phase Combustion:} Occurs via molecular diffusion in turbulent non-premixed flamelet structures governed by scalar dissipation and sub-grid mixing models (e.g., PaSR or EDC).
              \item \emph{Particle Combustion:} Requires a complex multiphase sequence: convective/radiative heating from the hot gas, melting ($T_m \approx 933\,\text{K}$), oxide shell ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $T_m \approx 2327\,\text{K}$) dissolution or mechanical rupture, metal vaporization ($\text{Al}_{(\text{l})} \to \text{Al}_{(\text{g})}$), and the formation of detached diffusion flame envelopes around individual droplets governed by the $d^n$-law, accompanied by condensed oxide smoke nucleation and interphase momentum/heat exchange.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Topology of Energy Release:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Gaseous afterburning is physically confined to the turbulent contact mixing layer at the outer boundary of the gaseous fireball.
              \item In contrast, ballistic aluminium particles can disperse throughout a wide volumetric zone ahead of the gaseous products, reacting directly with surrounding air and generating a distributed volumetric heat source that sustains the secondary blast overpressure and positive impulse over late time scales ($0.5\text{--}10\,\text{ms}$).
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Turbulence Modelling}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Large Eddy Simulation (LES):} Dynamic Smagorinsky or WALE subgrid-scale models resolve large-scale turbulent mixing structures without excessive artificial eddy viscosity. The filtered momentum equation introduces a subgrid stress tensor $\tau_{ij}^{\text{sgs}} = -2 \mu_t \bar{S}_{ij}$, where $\mu_t$ is computed dynamically from the resolved field. LES is particularly important for correctly predicting RMI and RTI driven mixing, which involves large coherent vortical structures at the product--air interface.
    \item \textbf{Reynolds-Averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS):} Compressible $k$-$\omega$ SST formulations with compressibility dilatational dissipation corrections (e.g., Sarkar model) provide computationally efficient approximations for 2D axisymmetric configurations. While RANS is less accurate for transient instability-driven mixing, it remains practical for parametric studies where LES cost is prohibitive.
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Turbulence-Chemistry Interaction (TCI)}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The fundamental challenge in turbulent combustion is that chemical reaction rates are nonlinear functions of temperature and species concentrations. The mean reaction rate $\overline{\dot{\omega}_k}$ cannot be evaluated simply at mean field values $\bar{T}$ and $\bar{Y}_k$; sub-grid fluctuations must be accounted for.
    \item In addition to pure Arrhenius finite-rate chemistry (which ignores sub-grid mixing), subgrid turbulent combustion models account for unmixedness and finite residence times within turbulent fine structures:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Partially Stirred Reactor (PaSR):} The effective reaction rate is reduced by a mixing efficiency factor $\kappa = \tau_c / (\tau_c + \tau_{\text{mix}})$, where $\tau_c$ is the chemical timescale and $\tau_{\text{mix}}$ is the turbulent mixing timescale. When $\tau_c \ll \tau_{\text{mix}}$ (mixing-controlled combustion), only a small fraction of the cell volume reacts at any instant.
              \item \emph{Eddy Dissipation Concept (EDC):} Models the fraction of the flow volume occupied by fine structures where reactions occur at near-stoichiometric conditions, providing a more rigorous treatment of the mixing-combustion coupling in highly turbulent environments.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================
\section{Aluminium Particle Modelling}
% ============================================================

The aluminium present in the explosive takes the form of particles with a distribution of sizes, typically ranging from sub-micron nano-Al to tens of microns for conventional micro-Al formulations. Particle size governs the interaction with the carrier gas (drag, heat transfer), the ignition delay, and the rate of combustion. Two principal approaches are used: Eulerian continuum descriptions, appropriate for the dense early-stage charge, and Lagrangian discrete element tracking, appropriate once the charge has expanded into a dilute dispersion.

% ---
\subsection{Eulerian Continuum Modelling}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Basic Volume-Fraction Method:} Solves continuum phase fraction $\alpha_{\text{Al}}$, velocity $\mathbf{u}_{\text{Al}}$, and temperature $T_{\text{Al}}$ fields using standard interphase drag (Gidaspow / Wen-Yu) and heat transfer (Ranz-Marshall / Gunn) correlations. The Eulerian approach treats the particle phase as an interpenetrating continuum, physically appropriate when particle number densities are high and individual particle-scale dynamics can be averaged over a representative volume.
    \item \textbf{High-Mach Compressible Interphase Exchange:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item The relative particle Mach number $\text{Ma}_p = \|\mathbf{u}_g - \mathbf{u}_p\| / c_g$ and particle Knudsen number $\text{Kn}_p \approx \sqrt{\gamma \pi / 2} (\text{Ma}_p / \text{Re}_p)$ must be considered explicitly. When $\text{Ma}_p \gtrsim 0.3$, standard drag correlations significantly overestimate drag forces because they do not account for the compressibility of the flow around the particle and the formation of a detached bow shock at $\text{Ma}_p > 1$.
              \item High-speed blast conditions therefore require compressible drag corrections such as the Henderson or Carlson--Hoglund models, which provide continuous drag correlations spanning the subsonic, transonic, and supersonic regimes as well as the continuum and free-molecular Knudsen number regimes.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Lagrangian Parcel Tracking}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Lagrangian tracking models individual aluminium particles or discrete numerical `parcels' (each representing a statistically representative ensemble of physical particles of the same size and state) by integrating equations of motion and heat transfer:
          \begin{equation}
              \frac{d\mathbf{u}_p}{dt} = \frac{\mathbf{F}_D}{m_p} + \mathbf{g}, \quad \frac{dT_p}{dt} = \frac{6}{\rho_p d_p c_p} \dot{q} + \frac{\dot{m}_p h_{\text{rxn}}}{m_p c_p}
          \end{equation}
          The drag force $\mathbf{F}_D = \frac{1}{2} C_D \rho_g \|\mathbf{u}_g - \mathbf{u}_p\|(\mathbf{u}_g - \mathbf{u}_p) \pi d_p^2 / 4$ depends on $C_D(\text{Re}_p, \text{Ma}_p)$, and the heat flux $\dot{q} = \pi d_p^2 h_{\text{conv}} (T_g - T_p)$ uses the Nusselt number $\text{Nu} = h_{\text{conv}} d_p / \lambda_g$ from the Ranz--Marshall correlation.
    \item \textbf{Advantages of the Lagrangian Approach:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Polydispersity:} Naturally handles arbitrary continuous particle size distributions (e.g., Rosin--Rammler or log-normal); each parcel carries its own diameter $d_p$, mass $m_p$, and composition.
              \item \emph{Particle History:} Enables exact tracking of trajectories, local residence times, thermal histories, Al melting, oxide cap growth, and surface reaction states along pathlines.
              \item \emph{No Numerical Diffusion:} Particle positions and velocity slips are resolved along exact trajectories, without the numerical smearing of Eulerian grid-based advection.
              \item \emph{Efficiency in Dilute Flows:} In expanding blast regimes ($\alpha_p \ll 1$), computational effort scales with the number of active parcels rather than the global mesh resolution.
              \item \emph{Stochastic Sub-Models:} Facilitates stochastic turbulent dispersion, parcel collision, and secondary aerodynamic break-up models.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drawback in Dense Regimes:} Standard Lagrangian parcel implementations assume dilute flow ($\alpha_p \approx 0$). In dense charges where aluminium occupies $\approx 20\text{--}35\%$ of initial volume, neglecting particle volume leads to significant underestimation of initial charge density and large errors in early blast pressures.
    \item \textbf{Potential Extension for Finite Particle Volume (Dense DPM / MP-PIC):}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Coupled Governing Equations:} Incorporates the gas volume fraction $\epsilon_g = 1 - \theta_p$ directly into the compressible carrier-phase equations:
                    \begin{equation}
                        \frac{\partial (\epsilon_g \rho_g)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\epsilon_g \rho_g \mathbf{u}_g) = \dot{m}_{\text{transfer}}
                    \end{equation}
                    \begin{equation}
                        \frac{\partial (\epsilon_g \rho_g \mathbf{u}_g)}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\epsilon_g \rho_g \mathbf{u}_g \mathbf{u}_g) = -\epsilon_g \nabla p + \nabla \cdot (\epsilon_g \boldsymbol{\tau}_g) + \mathbf{S}_{M,p}
                    \end{equation}
                    accompanied by particle contact stress models (e.g., Harris--Crighton granular pressure) to prevent packing beyond the random close-packing limit ($\theta_{p,\text{max}} \approx 0.63\text{--}0.65$).
              \item \emph{Mesh Resolution Sensitivity:} Under AMR or fine grids near shock fronts, cell sizes approach parcel volumes, causing severe grid dependency and high-frequency noise in $\epsilon_g$ that excites spurious acoustic waves.
              \item \emph{Spatial Smoothing Kernels:} Projecting parcel volumes and source terms onto the Eulerian grid via Gaussian or polynomial filter functions spanning multiple cells mitigates grid dependency, but increases parallel MPI overhead due to non-local kernel contributions.
              \item \emph{Riemann Solver Compatibility:} Variable $\epsilon_g(\mathbf{x}, t)$ alters the characteristic wave structure of the compressible system; shock-capturing schemes must be reformulated to handle volume-fraction-weighted fluxes without generating spurious oscillations across steep $\nabla \theta_p$ interfaces.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Population Balance Methods}

The single-size Eulerian approach cannot inherently capture the polydisperse nature of aluminium size distributions. Population balance methods extend the Eulerian framework to track the evolution of the full particle size distribution $n(d, \mathbf{x}, t)$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Discrete Sectional Method (MUSIG):} Discretizes the size distribution into finite bins (e.g., $d_i \in [1, 5, 10, 20, 50]\,\mu\text{m}$) using \texttt{diameterModels/reacting}. Separate continuity equations are solved with source terms for burning, break-up, and agglomeration. Computational cost scales linearly with bin count; multi-velocity formulations (where each bin has a distinct velocity) capture size-dependent inertial transport but are particularly expensive.
    \item \emph{Quadrature Method of Moments (QMOM):} Transports statistical moments of the number density function $n(d)$:
          \begin{equation}
              m_k = \int_0^\infty d^k \, n(d) \, \mathrm{d}d \quad (k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, 2N-1)
          \end{equation}
          where $m_0$ is the total particle number density and $m_1/m_0$ is the mean diameter. The transport equation for the $k$-th moment is:
          \begin{equation}
              \frac{\partial m_k}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (m_k \mathbf{u}_{\text{Al}}) = \sum_{\alpha=1}^N w_\alpha \, k \, d_\alpha^{k-1} \left(\frac{d(d_p)}{dt}\right)_\alpha + \dot{S}_{m_k,\text{break/agg}}
          \end{equation}
          Weights $w_\alpha$ and abscissas $d_\alpha$ are dynamically computed from lower-order moments via the product-difference (PD) or Wheeler algorithm. QMOM transports only $2N$ scalar fields (typically $N = 3\text{--}4$) to represent the full distribution, offering much lower overhead than multi-bin MUSIG.
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Eulerian-to-Lagrangian Transfer}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rationale for a Hybrid Approach:} Within the dense initial charge ($\alpha_p \approx 0.2\text{--}0.35$), the Eulerian continuum approach is physically appropriate and avoids the overhead of tracking vast numbers of densely-packed parcels. Once the charge has expanded sufficiently, transitioning to Lagrangian parcels allows each particle's combustion history to be tracked individually without the numerical diffusion of Eulerian advection.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Strategy:} Eulerian formulation is used in the dense, early expansion phase ($\alpha_p > \alpha_{\text{crit}}$), transitioning to Lagrangian parcels in dilute regions ($\alpha_p \le \alpha_{\text{crit}} \approx 10^{-3}$). The critical volume fraction is chosen at the point where the continuum assumption breaks down and individual particle tracking becomes both feasible and necessary.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Seeding Algorithm:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Eulerian mass $m_{\text{cell}} = \alpha_p \rho_p V_{\text{cell}}$ is converted to discrete Lagrangian parcels when dilute criteria are met. The number of parcels per cell is chosen to achieve a target number of physical particles per parcel.
              \item Parcel diameters are sampled from the local Eulerian size distribution or QMOM moment state. Parcel velocities and temperatures are initialised from the local Eulerian phase fields, ensuring continuity of momentum and energy across the transition.
              \item Eulerian phase fields are decremented with strict conservation of momentum and energy; the Eulerian phase fraction is set to zero and the newly created parcels carry the full momentum and thermal content of the removed material.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Aluminium Combustion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Phase Transitions and Ignition}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Aluminium undergoes melting at $T_{\text{melt}} = 933\,\text{K}$ ($\Delta H_{\text{fus}} \approx 397\,\text{kJ/kg}$) and vaporization at $T_{\text{boil}} \approx 2792\,\text{K}$ ($\Delta H_{\text{vap}} \approx 10.5\,\text{MJ/kg}$). During melting, absorbed latent heat causes the particle temperature to plateau at $T_{\text{melt}}$ until all solid Al has liquefied. This is accounted for by introducing an effective heat capacity $c_{p,\text{eff}} = c_p + \Delta H_{\text{fus}} \delta(T - T_{\text{melt}})$ in the particle energy equation.
    \item The passivation oxide shell ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) breaks down when particle surface temperatures approach the alumina melting point ($T_{\text{ign}} \approx 2050\text{--}2300\,\text{K}$). Below this threshold the shell acts as a diffusion barrier suppressing combustion. Once the oxide shell melts, liquid Al is exposed directly to the oxidising environment and combustion initiates rapidly. The spread in ignition temperatures reflects sensitivity to particle heating rate, surface contaminants, and local oxidizer species.
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Combustion Regimes and Burn-Rate Law}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Micro-Al ($d > 5\,\mu\text{m}$):} Governed by detached vapour-phase diffusion flame envelopes around burning Al droplets with exponent $n \approx 1.8\text{--}2.0$. Al first vaporizes at the liquid surface and combustion occurs in a surrounding flame zone where Al vapour and oxidizer meet. Burn time scales as $t_b \propto d_p^n / K_{\text{comb}}$, analogous to the classical $d^2$-law for hydrocarbon droplets.
    \item \emph{Nano-Al ($d < 1\,\mu\text{m}$):} Dominated by heterogeneous surface chemical kinetics with $n \approx 1.0\text{--}1.2$. The high surface-area-to-volume ratio means combustion occurs primarily as a surface reaction; burn time becomes linearly proportional to particle diameter.
    \item \emph{Beckstead Multi-Oxidizer Empirical Law:} In oxygen-depleted blast fireballs, combustion rates scale with effective oxidizer mole fractions:
          \begin{equation}
              \frac{d(d_p)}{dt} = -\frac{2 K_{\text{comb}}}{\rho_p d_p^{n-1}} X_{\text{eff}}^{0.9} \left(1 + 0.276\,\text{Re}^{1/2}\text{Sc}^{1/3}\right)
          \end{equation}
          where $X_{\text{eff}} = X_{\text{O}_2} + 0.6 X_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} + 0.22 X_{\text{CO}_2}$ is the effective oxidizer mole fraction, and the convective correction accounts for enhanced mass transfer due to relative particle--gas motion.
    \item \emph{Pressure-Dependent Empirical Burn-Rate Law and Physical Calibration:} In high-pressure detonation and early blast expansion environments, aluminium surface regression is parameterized via the Saint-Robert / Vieille empirical pressure law implemented in \texttt{blastEulerFoam}'s \texttt{diameterModels/reacting} library:
          \begin{equation}
              r_b(p) = \begin{cases}
                  \text{offset} + a \left(p \cdot p_{\text{scale}}\right)^n, & p \ge p_{\text{min}} \\
                  0, & p < p_{\text{min}}
              \end{cases}
          \end{equation}
          where $r_b(p)$ is the linear surface regression rate ($\text{m/s}$), $p$ is the local fluid pressure, $p_{\text{scale}} = 10^{-6}\,\text{Pa}^{-1}$ normalizes pressure into megapascals ($\text{MPa}$), $n$ is the empirical pressure exponent, $a$ is the pre-exponential rate coefficient ($\text{m/s}$), and $p_{\text{min}}$ is a physical activation/ignition pressure threshold. Under spherical particle geometry, the diameter regression rate obeys:
          \begin{equation}
              \frac{d(d_p)}{dt} = -2\,r_b(p) = -2\left[\text{offset} + a \left(p \cdot p_{\text{scale}}\right)^n\right]
          \end{equation}
          subject to the non-negative diameter floor $d_p \ge 0$.
    \item \emph{Timescale Separation: Detonation vs.\ Afterburning:} In aluminized high explosives (e.g., Composition~B / Al, Tritonal, PBXN-109), atomized aluminium powders typically possess median diameters $d_0 \approx 20\text{--}40\,\mu\text{m}$ (nominal $d_0 = 30\,\mu\text{m}$). Physical aluminium combustion is governed by vapour diffusion flame envelopes with total burn times $t_{\text{burn}} \approx d_0^{1.8\text{--}2.0}/K_{\text{comb}} \sim 0.5\text{--}1.5\,\text{ms}$ ($500\text{--}1500\,\mu\text{s}$). Consequently, during the microsecond Chapman--Jouguet detonation wave transit ($t \le 20\,\mu\text{s}$), aluminium particles are largely unburned, acting primarily as dense mechanical mass ballast and a thermal heat sink that slightly reduces CJ velocity (from $\sim 8000\,\text{m/s}$ to $\sim 7655\,\text{m/s}$). The vast majority of aluminium chemical heat release ($31\,\text{MJ/kg}$) is liberated downstream during late-time fireball expansion and turbulent afterburning ($t \sim 0.2\text{--}5\,\text{ms}$).
          
          To avoid unphysical instantaneous consumption where particles vanish in microseconds, the empirical burn-rate law is calibrated to realistic physical scales ($d_0 = 30\,\mu\text{m}$, $\text{offset} = 0$, $p_{\text{scale}} = 10^{-6}\,\text{Pa}^{-1}$, $n = 0.7$, $a = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{m/s}$, and $p_{\text{min}} = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\text{Pa}$). At peak detonation pressures ($p \approx 14\text{--}20\,\text{GPa}$), this yields a physical surface regression rate $r_b \approx 26.2\,\text{mm/s}$, producing a partial core regression of $\Delta d_p \approx 0.76\,\mu\text{m}$ ($2.5\%$ diameter reduction, $\approx 7\%$ volumetric consumption) over the initial $20\,\mu\text{s}$. This regression rate accurately corresponds to a total physical burn-out duration $t_{\text{burn}} = d_0 / (2 r_b) \approx 0.57\,\text{ms}$, while strictly preventing spurious burning in unshocked ambient air ($p < p_{\text{min}}$).
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Heterogeneous Reaction Channels and Kinetics}

The principal oxidation reactions of aluminium with available oxidizer species in detonating mixtures are:
\begin{align}
    4\text{Al}_{(\text{s,l})} + 3\text{O}_2         & \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3              \quad (\Delta H_R = -31.0\,\text{MJ/kg}_{\text{Al}}) \\
    2\text{Al}_{(\text{s,l})} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} & \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{H}_2 \quad (\Delta H_R = -15.4\,\text{MJ/kg}_{\text{Al}}) \\
    2\text{Al}_{(\text{s,l})} + 3\text{CO}_2        & \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{CO}  \quad (\Delta H_R = -13.6\,\text{MJ/kg}_{\text{Al}})
\end{align}
The reactions with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CO}_2$ are particularly significant in aluminized explosive fireballs, as these species are abundant detonation products and sustain aluminium combustion even in anaerobic interior regions where atmospheric $\text{O}_2$ has not yet penetrated. 

In addition to heterogeneous particle regression, secondary gas-phase chemical reactions release substantial energy as combustible intermediates mix with entrained ambient air:
\begin{align}
    2\text{CO} + \text{O}_2  & \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2  \quad (\Delta H_R = -10.1\,\text{MJ/kg}_{\text{CO}})  \\
    2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 & \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\Delta H_R = -121.0\,\text{MJ/kg}_{\text{H}_2}) \\
    \text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 & \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\Delta H_R = -50.0\,\text{MJ/kg}_{\text{CH}_4})
\end{align}
In \texttt{blastEulerFoam}, gas-phase reaction rates $\dot{\omega}_k$ are integrated via finite-rate Arrhenius kinetics coupled to stiff ODE solvers (\texttt{RKCK45} / \texttt{EulerImplicit}), tracking dynamic volumetric heat release $\dot{Q}_{\text{gas}} = -\sum_k \dot{\omega}_k \Delta h_{f,k}^\circ$ in the energy equation.

% ---
\subsection{Post-Combustion Condensation of Aluminium Oxide}
\label{sec:alumina_condensation}

In high-temperature aluminium combustion, the primary chemical product in the high-temperature flame zone ($T \sim 3000\text{--}4000\,\text{K}$) is vapour-phase sub-oxides ($\text{AlO}, \text{Al}_2\text{O}, \text{AlO}_2$) and gaseous alumina ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{g})}$). As the product plume expands, cools, and mixes with cooler ambient air, these vapour species undergo rapid post-combustion phase change, transitioning through liquid droplets ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{l})}$) and ultimately solidifying into solid alumina ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{s})}$):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Condensation Temperatures and Phase Boundaries:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item Gaseous alumina condenses to liquid droplets below the alumina condensation/boiling temperature ($T_{\text{cond}} \approx 3800\text{--}4100\,\text{K}$ at elevated blast pressures; $\approx 3250\,\text{K}$ at atmospheric pressure).
              \item Liquid alumina droplets freeze into solid particulate smoke ($\alpha\text{--}\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 / \gamma\text{--}\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) at the alumina melting point ($T_{\text{melt, alumina}} \approx 2327\,\text{K}$).
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Massive Latent Heat Release:}
          Alumina condensation is intensely exothermic. The latent heat of condensation is $\Delta H_{\text{cond}} \approx 18.0\text{--}19.2\,\text{MJ/kg}$ (relative to $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$), and the latent heat of fusion is $\Delta H_{\text{fus, alumina}} \approx 1.1\,\text{MJ/kg}$.
          \begin{equation}
              \text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{g})} \xrightarrow{\Delta H_{\text{cond}} \approx 18.6\,\text{MJ/kg}} \text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{l})} \xrightarrow{\Delta H_{\text{fus}} \approx 1.1\,\text{MJ/kg}} \text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{s})}
          \end{equation}
          Because the latent heat of condensation represents over $\approx 55\text{--}60\%$ of the total chemical enthalpy released during aluminium oxidation ($31.0\,\text{MJ/kg}$ Al), post-combustion condensation serves as a massive thermal reservoir in the expanding fireball. This thermal buffering locks local gas temperatures near the phase transition plateaus ($T \approx 2300\text{--}3800\,\text{K}$), suppressing rapid thermal collapse and providing sustained thermal expansion that enhances the late-time quasi-steady blast impulse.
    \item \textbf{Nucleation, Smoke Formation, and Cap Accumulation:}
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Ultrafine Aerosol Smoke:} Homogeneous vapour-to-liquid nucleation in the detached flame envelope generates a dense aerosol cloud of sub-micron alumina particles ($d_{\text{smoke}} \approx 10\text{--}200\,\text{nm}$).
              \item \emph{Oxide Cap Formation and Droplet Dynamics:} A fraction of the condensed liquid alumina deposits directly onto the parent burning droplet surface, forming liquid oxide lobes or "caps". As the droplet burns, the growing oxide cap alters aerodynamic drag, induces droplet spinning, and can trigger micro-explosions or asymmetric droplet fragmentation.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radiative and Thermodynamic Coupling in \texttt{blastFoam}:}
          In the multicomponent thermodynamics database (\texttt{thermo.compressibleGas}), condensed alumina is explicitly tracked as condensed-phase species ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{s})}$ / $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{l})}$) with temperature-dependent JANAF enthalpy polynomials. This ensures that the enormous latent heat of condensation is correctly coupled into the carrier-phase energy equation rather than lost as a numerical artefact. Furthermore, the resulting sub-micron alumina smoke cloud provides the high optical absorption and emission coefficients ($\kappa_p$) that govern the radiative transport solver in \texttt{blastRadFoam}.
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Thermal Radiation}

Sub-micron condensed-phase $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ smoke particles produced as combustion products have high emissivity ($\epsilon \approx 0.9$) and make thermal radiation a significant energy loss mechanism from the fireball, particularly at peak combustion temperatures of $3000\text{--}4000\,\text{K}$ where blackbody emission peaks in the near-infrared.

\texttt{blastRadFoam} solves the radiative transport equation (RTE) via either the P1 approximation or the finite volume discrete ordinates method (fvDOM). P1 is computationally efficient and suitable for optically thick media where the photon mean free path is small compared to the domain size. The fvDOM solves the full directional RTE along a discrete set of solid angle directions and is more accurate for optically thin regions or where directional effects are significant, at higher computational cost:
\begin{equation}
    -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{q}_{\text{rad}} = \kappa_p \left(4 \pi I_b(T_g) - G\right)
\end{equation}
where $G = \int_{4\pi} I \, d\Omega$ is incident radiation integrated over all solid angles, $I_b(T_g) = \sigma T_g^4 / \pi$ is the blackbody intensity, and $\kappa_p = \frac{3}{2}\frac{\alpha_{\text{smoke}} Q_{\text{abs}}}{d_{\text{smoke}}}$ is the Planck mean absorption coefficient of the smoke particle cloud, with $Q_{\text{abs}}$ the absorption efficiency factor from Mie theory.

\section{Numerical Methods}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Riemann Solvers and Flux Schemes}

High-order shock-capturing is achieved using a selection of Godunov-type Riemann solvers with runtime-configurable flux scheme selectors (\texttt{NewSingle} / \texttt{NewMulti}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HLLC:} Exactly resolves the contact wave and shear waves in addition to acoustic waves. Preferred for single-phase regions where wave structure is well-defined.
    \item \textbf{AUSM+:} Provides good accuracy for both low-Mach number and supersonic flows and handles the carbuncle instability more robustly than pure upwind schemes.
    \item \textbf{Kurganov--Tadmor (KT):} A central-upwind scheme that is straightforward to extend to multiphase and non-conservative formulations. Used as the default for multiphase configurations.
    \item \textbf{TVD/MUSCL Limiters:} 2nd-order reconstruction using van Leer or Minmod limiters, which switch between 1st-order (diffusive but stable) and 2nd-order (accurate but prone to oscillations) reconstructions based on local gradient smoothness.
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Adaptive Mesh Refinement}

Dynamic octree/2D mesh refinement maintains computational efficiency by concentrating resolution only where it is physically needed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Refinement is guided by normalised pressure gradients $\|\nabla p\| / p$, density and phase gradients $\|\nabla \rho\|$ and $\|\nabla \alpha_{\text{Al}}\|$, and temperature gradients $\|\nabla T\|$. Cells exceeding user-specified thresholds are subdivided; cells below a coarsening threshold in smooth regions are merged.
    \item Refinement is typically limited to 2--4 levels, with each level halving the local cell size, giving effective refinement ratios of 4--16$\times$ relative to the base mesh. This dramatically reduces total cell count compared to a uniformly fine mesh while still resolving sub-millimeter shock structures.
    \item AMR interacts directly with Lagrangian parcel tracking: when AMR refines cells near particle-laden regions, the spatial filter kernels used for volume projection must be recomputed, introducing additional parallel communication overhead.
\end{itemize}

% ---
\subsection{Numerical Stability Improvements}

Several targeted stability fixes were implemented to prevent solver divergence in extreme multiphase conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Microscopic Phase Fraction Cutoff:} In \texttt{twoPhaseCompressibleSystem::decode()}, a volume fraction cutoff of $10^{-15}$ prevents unphysical EOS evaluations in void cells. Without this guard, near-zero phase fractions can trigger divisions by near-zero densities, leading to NaN propagation and solver divergence.
    \item \emph{Conservative Phase Decode in \texttt{blastEulerFoam}:}\label{sec:conservative_decode} The phase \texttt{decode()} functions modify the conserved variables ($\alpha\rho$, $\alpha\rho\mathbf{u}$, $\alpha\rho E$) only where a phase is residual ($\alpha \le$ \texttt{residualAlpha}); velocity caps and temperature-range clamps act on the primitive variables only. An earlier density/velocity blending and energy reset for $\alpha < 0.01$ destroyed $1\text{--}2\%$ of the energy in the 0D transfer tests and deleted the energy of the products expanding into the air. Removing it exposed two causes of spurious GPa pressures that it had masked. First, the granular buoyancy term was built from the individual fluid-phase pressure gradients, which are undefined where a phase is absent; it now uses $-\alpha_s \nabla p$ with the mixture fluid pressure. Second, trace fluid phases ($\alpha \sim 10^{-5}\text{--}10^{-2}$) at fluid/fluid contacts acquire unreliable densities $\alpha\rho/\alpha$, because without pressure relaxation all fluid phases in a cell undergo the same volumetric strain. Dilute fluid phases ($\alpha < 0.01$) are therefore brought towards pressure equilibrium with the carrier phase by a damped Newton volume exchange, $\Delta\alpha = (p_k - p_c)/(\rho_k c_k^2/\alpha_k + \rho_c c_c^2/\alpha_c)$, with the work $\bar p\,\Delta\alpha$ exchanged between the two phase energies, so mass, momentum and total energy are conserved. Compression of the dilute phase is limited to a factor of two per step, to keep it within the range of its equation of state. The decoded fluid density is no longer bounded below by \texttt{residualRho} (that bound held expanded gas at $1.2\,\text{kg/m}^3$ and overstated its pressure up to twentyfold), and where a granular phase would exceed $\alpha_\text{max}$ its mass is kept; the packing limit is enforced by the frictional stress, which requires a small \texttt{alphaDeltaMin} ($10^{-3}$ in the Comp-B/Al cases). In cells where the contact has numerically mixed products and air, the inert and reacting Comp-B/Al cases equilibrate the volumes of all non-carrier fluid phases (\texttt{alphaDilute 1}) and relax the products and gas temperatures with a stiff constant-coefficient heat transfer; the exponential update is exact and conservative. Without the temperature relaxation, products that are a trace by mass reach $10^5\,\text{K}$ next to cold compressed air; with it, the mixed cells are in pressure and temperature equilibrium (e.g.\ $9.7\,\text{MPa}$, $3435\,\text{K}$ at $40\,\mu\text{s}$). The Al-combustion case keeps the dilute-only setting, because its burning Al releases mass into a trace gas phase inside the detonating charge that the stronger relaxation drives outside the gas equation-of-state range. A stationary unreacted charge/air interface case (\path{tests/1D_spherical/ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_interface}) checks that the interface stays at rest. The near-field air shock changes substantially: at $100\,\text{mm}$ it arrives after $17.5\,\mu\text{s}$ with $17\,\text{MPa}$ (previously $25.4\,\mu\text{s}$ and $90\,\text{MPa}$). Pressure relaxation in non-dilute mixed cells remains open (\path{report/dilute_phase_conservation_plan.md}).
    \item \emph{Species Mass Fraction Correction:} The mixture mass fraction normalisation routine was overhauled. The updated code (in \texttt{basicSpecieBlastMixture}) handles zero-density cells ($\sum Y_i \approx 0$) by resetting to the designated default species without aborting.
    \item \emph{Pressure and Temperature Bounds:} Maximum pressure thresholds ($p_{\text{max}} = 10^{15}\,\text{Pa}$) were implemented across JWL, Murnaghan, and perfect gas models. Temperature-clamped evaluations in \texttt{janafBlastThermo} prevent divergence outside the valid fitting range ($200\text{--}6000\,\text{K}$) during transient shock passage.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Canonical Unit Physics \& Riemann Solver Benchmarks}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{0D Isochoric Phase Transfer Reactors}\\

\subsection{Stage 1: 0D Unit Physics \& Reaction Kinetics Verification}

% ------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{WP 1.1: 0D Isochoric Phase Transfer Reactors (\path{tests/0D/phaseTransfer_0D}, \path{tests/0D/phaseTransfer_0D_3phase}):}

Each phase carries its own mass, momentum and total energy, and the transferred mass carries the source velocity and total energy (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_conservation}). The \texttt{EuDetCode} CJ temperature ($T_{\text{CJ}} = 3432.62\,\text{K}$) is retained, and the temperature exponent is set to $\omega_T = 0.3555$ (instead of $\omega = 0.2387$) so that the products start at the gas-EOS temperature of the transfer state, $T_0 = 825.76\,\text{K}$; this changes only the products temperature scale, not their pressure or energy. No energy offset is applied ($e_{\text{offset}} = 0$); the datums are reconciled through $E_0$.

The gas is formed at $830.62\,\text{K}$ and the pressure rises monotonically to $100.58\,\text{bar}$ (maximum variation $0.58\%$; the WP~1.1 target of $0.01\%$ is not met). The total energy is conserved to $4.7 \times 10^{-9}$ ($5.2 \times 10^{-9}$ with aluminium) over the whole run, including the last $9\,\mu\text{s}$ in which the products phase is dilute ($\alpha < 0.01$). Before the conservative phase decode (Section~\ref{sec:conservative_decode}), a reset of the dilute products phase to $288.15\,\text{K}$ removed $1.81\%$ ($1.10\%$) of the energy at $t = 91\,\mu\text{s}$.

          In the earlier single-energy \texttt{blastFoam} version of this test, where the phases share one temperature, $\omega_T = \omega$ placed the products at $1318.69\,\text{K}$; the shared temperature then relaxed by $\approx 484\,\text{K}$, giving a $16.5\%$ pressure excursion with the composition-based products heat capacity ($C_v = 1450\,\text{J/(kg\,K)}$). At the transfer state the excluded co-volume factor is modest ($b_{\text{mix}}\rho = 0.0482$, $95.2\%$ free volume).
    \item \textbf{WP 1.2, 1.4, 1.5: Aluminium Particle Surface Aerodynamics, Goodman HBIM Heating \& Combustion Kinetics}\\
          (\path{tests/0D/verify_al_particle_kinetics.py}):
          Under the intense convective heating rates characteristic of detonation products and shock waves ($h_{\text{conv}} \sim 10^4\text{--}10^5\,\text{W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}$), the classical assumption of a uniform internal particle temperature (lumped capacitance) becomes physically invalid. Because the internal thermal diffusion timescale $\tau_d \sim R^2/\alpha_p$ is finite, steep temperature gradients $\partial T/\partial r$ develop across the particle radius $R = d_p/2$. Since oxide shell rupture and ignition are strictly local surface phenomena governed by the outer surface temperature $T_s(t)$, assuming a uniform bulk temperature mispredicts and artificially delays ignition.

          To resolve this, a spherical particle surface heating model based on the \textbf{Goodman Heat-Balance Integral Method (HBIM)} was implemented. The radial heat conduction equation in spherical coordinates:
          \begin{equation}
              \rho_p c_p \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \left( k_p r^2 \frac{\partial T}{\partial r} \right)
          \end{equation}
          is subject to the convective Robin boundary condition at the outer surface $r = R$:
          \begin{equation}
              k_p \left. \frac{\partial T}{\partial r} \right|_{r=R} = h_{\text{conv}} \left( T_g - T_s(t) \right)
          \end{equation}
          and symmetry at the centre $\left. \frac{\partial T}{\partial r} \right|_{r=0} = 0$. The transient heating process is divided into two distinct physical regimes:
          \begin{enumerate}
              \item \emph{Phase 1: Inward Thermal Penetration ($\delta(t) \le R$):} A thermal boundary layer of thickness $\delta(t)$ penetrates inward from $r = R$. For $r < R - \delta(t)$, the core remains undisturbed at ambient temperature $T(r, t) = T_0$. Within the thermal layer $R - \delta \le r \le R$, the temperature profile is represented by Goodman's parabolic ansatz:
                    \begin{equation}
                        T(r, t) - T_0 = \left( T_s(t) - T_0 \right) \left( \frac{r - (R - \delta(t))}{\delta(t)} \right)^2
                    \end{equation}
                    Evaluating the surface gradient and substituting into the Robin condition yields the surface temperature relation:
                    \begin{equation}
                        T_s(t) - T_0 = \left( T_g - T_0 \right) \frac{\text{Bi}_\delta}{2 + \text{Bi}_\delta}, \quad \text{Bi}_\delta = \frac{h_{\text{conv}} \delta(t)}{k_p}
                    \end{equation}
                    Integrating the governing PDE over the shell $[R - \delta(t), R]$ gives the volumetric thermal moment ODE governing the penetration rate $d\delta/dt$, reaching full penetration ($\delta = R$) at $t_{\text{pen}} \sim \mathcal{O}(R^2 / (6\alpha_p))$.
              \item \emph{Phase 2: Post-Penetration Profile ($\delta = R$):} Once the thermal wave reaches the centre, the temperature throughout the sphere evolves as:
                    \begin{equation}
                        T(r, t) = T_c(t) + \left( T_s(t) - T_c(t) \right) \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2
                    \end{equation}
                    where the surface temperature excess satisfies $T_s(t) - T_c(t) = (T_g - T_c(t)) \frac{\text{Bi}_R}{2 + \text{Bi}_R}$ with $\text{Bi}_R = h_{\text{conv}} R / k_p$. Integrating total energy over the sphere yields an exact linear ODE for the core temperature $T_c(t)$:
                    \begin{equation}
                        \frac{dT_c}{dt} = \frac{T_g - T_c}{\tau_{\text{eff}}}, \quad \tau_{\text{eff}} = \frac{m_p c_p}{h_{\text{conv}} A_p} + \frac{R^2}{15 \alpha_p}
                    \end{equation}
                    where the second term $\frac{R^2}{15\alpha_p}$ represents the internal conductive diffusion lag.
          \end{enumerate}

          As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_al_kinetics}(a), under representative blast slip conditions ($h_{\text{conv}} = 3.0 \times 10^5\,\text{W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}, k_{\text{eff}} = 6.0\,\text{W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}, \text{Bi}_R = 0.500$), the Goodman HBIM surface temperature $T_s(t)$ rises significantly faster than the volume-averaged temperature $T_{\text{avg}}(t)$ and the classical lumped model $T_{\text{lump}}(t)$. Consequently, surface melting ($T_{\text{melt}} = 933\,\text{K}$) occurs at $t = 5.8\,\mu\text{s}$ (when the core is still cold at $467\,\text{K}$) and oxide shell rupture/ignition ($T_{\text{ign}} = 2050\,\text{K}$) occurs substantially earlier ($t_{\text{ign, Goodman}} = 32.9\,\mu\text{s}$ vs.\ $t_{\text{ign, lump}} = 38.9\,\mu\text{s}$ for a $20\,\mu\text{m}$ particle, a $15.5\%$ delay reduction). Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_al_kinetics}(b) illustrates the reconstructed internal temperature profiles $T(r/R)$ across successive time snapshots, resolving the steep radial temperature drop ($\Delta T = 187\text{--}500\,\text{K}$) and the inward-propagating thermal penetration front $\delta(t)$ during Phase 1 ($t \le 3.7\,\mu\text{s}$) before transitioning to the parabolic profile during Phase 2.

          Following ignition, particle diameter regression follows the Beckstead multi-oxidizer $d^{1.9}$ rate law:
          \begin{equation}
              \frac{d(d_p)}{dt} = -\frac{2 K_{\text{comb}}}{\rho_p d_p^{0.9}} \left(X_{\text{O}_2} + 0.6 X_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} + 0.22 X_{\text{CO}_2}\right)^{0.9} \left(1 + 0.276 \text{Re}_p^{1/2} \text{Sc}^{1/3}\right)
          \end{equation}
          Crucially, the exothermic condensation of alumina vapour ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{g})} \to \text{Al}_2\text{O}_{3(\text{s})}$) releases an additional $19.70\,\text{MJ/kg}$, elevating total energy release to $50.70\,\text{MJ/kg}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_al_kinetics}(c)) and preventing rapid thermal collapse of the blast fireball.
    \item \textbf{WP 1.3: High-Temperature Air Thermodynamics \& Equilibrium Dissociation (\path{tests/0D/verify_highT_air_thermo.py}):}
          NASA-9 polynomial evaluations were verified across temperatures from $300\,\text{K}$ to $6000\,\text{K}$. At $300\,\text{K}$, ambient air ratio of specific heats $\gamma = 1.400$ is recovered. Vibrational excitation reduces $\gamma$ to $1.28$ at $3000\,\text{K}$, while endothermic dissociation of oxygen ($\text{O}_2 \leftrightarrow 2\text{O}$) and nitrogen ($\text{N}_2 \leftrightarrow 2\text{N}$) was verified against JANAF/CEA equilibrium constants.

          (\path{tests/1D/shock_dissociating_air}, \path{tests/1D/verify_shock_dissociating_air.py}):

    \item \textbf{WP 2.2: 1D Multiphase Interface Advection}\\

\subsection{Stage 3: 1D Spherical Blast Expansion, Kingery--Bulmash Scaling \& Multiphase Detonation}
\label{sec:stage3_results}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Stage 3 evaluated \texttt{blastFoam} and \texttt{blastEulerFoam}'s capacity to model multidimensional blast expansion, air shock attenuation, and spherical multiphase detonations using computationally lightweight 1D spherical sector configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1D Spherical Blast Wave Expansion \& Kingery--Bulmash Validation (WP 3.1):}\\
          The domain encompasses a spherical sector spanning $R = 6.0\,\text{m}$ with 300 radial cells ($\Delta r = 20\,\text{mm}$), bounded by four lateral wedge boundaries meeting at the origin and an unconstrained transmissive outer boundary. A $1.0\,\text{kg}$ spherical C4 explosive charge ($R_0 = 0.05304\,\text{m}, \rho = 1601\,\text{kg/m}^3$) was initiated at the origin using programmed burn with the \texttt{JWL} equation of state ($E_0 = 9.0 \times 10^9\,\text{Pa}, v_{\text{det}} = 7850\,\text{m/s}$), expanding into quiescent ambient air ($p_0 = 101298\,\text{Pa}, T_0 = 288\,\text{K}$). Execution required only $12.4\,\text{s}$ of CPU time for a $5.0\,\text{ms}$ physical duration.
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Peak Overpressure Attenuation:} Incident peak overpressure history was captured across the entire domain using the newly introduced \texttt{temporalMax} Action (tracking all 300 cells along the radial wedge continuum across scaled distances $Z = 0.35\text{--}3.5\,\text{m/kg}^{1/3}$). As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_blast}(a), the continuous overpressure attenuation exhibits strict monotonic decay across all arriving stations: $35.83\,\text{bar}$ at $0.5\,\text{m}$, $8.47\,\text{bar}$ at $1.0\,\text{m}$, $3.23\,\text{bar}$ at $1.5\,\text{m}$, $1.69\,\text{bar}$ at $2.0\,\text{m}$, and $0.74\,\text{bar}$ at $3.0\,\text{m}$. The fine-grained 300-cell profile matches the empirical Kingery--Bulmash and Kinney--Graham standards across the entire shock expansion range, confirming accurate spatial decay capture without artificial dispersion or probe spatial aliasing.
              \item \emph{Positive Phase Impulse \& Waveform Structure:} To capture continuous impulse distributions, a companion \texttt{temporalIntegral} function object was developed to integrate arbitrary scalar fields over time ($I_+(\mathbf{x}) = \int \max(p(\mathbf{x}, t) - p_0, 0)\,\mathrm{d}t$) with volume-weighted coarse-mesh accumulation across adaptive mesh refinement. The positive phase impulse $I_+(Z)$ is continuously mapped across all 300 cells (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_blast}(b)), resolving the near-field non-monotonic impulse peak and tracking the empirical Kingery--Bulmash curve with remarkable fidelity: $3.41\%$ relative error at $Z = 1.0\,\text{m/kg}^{1/3}$ ($157.24$ vs.\ $152.08\,\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$), $8.00\%$ at $Z = 2.0\,\text{m/kg}^{1/3}$ ($85.18$ vs.\ $92.59\,\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$), and $19.53\%$ at $Z = 3.0\,\text{m/kg}^{1/3}$ ($56.13$ vs.\ $69.75\,\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$). As illustrated in the gauge pressure waveforms (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_blast}(c)), \texttt{blastFoam} resolves sharp, discontinuous shock front arrivals followed by physical exponential Friedlander relaxation and underpressure suction.
          \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1D Spherical Multiphase CJ Detonation: Inert Baseline, Reacting Gas \& Empirical Al Combustion (WP 3.2):}\\
          To evaluate Eulerian--Eulerian two-fluid detonation dynamics in spherical coordinates, a three-tier comparative suite of 1D spherical configurations was formulated and benchmarked in \texttt{blastEulerFoam}: the inert baseline (\path{tests/1D_spherical/ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical}), the reacting gas formulation (\path{tests/1D_spherical/ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_reacting}), and the full particulate aluminium combustion model (\path{tests/1D_spherical/ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_alCombustion}):
          \begin{itemize}
              \item \emph{Physical Setup and Discretization:} The spherical wedge domain ($L = 0.20\,\text{m}$, $400$ radial wedge cells, $\Delta r = 0.5\,\text{mm}$) models an origin-initiated spherical charge ($r \le 0.05\,\text{m}$) of aluminized Composition~B ($70\%$ Comp-B / $30\%$ Al) surrounded by ambient air. In the Eulerian two-fluid formulation, solid particulate aluminium ($\alpha_{\text{Al}} = 0.206$) and gaseous detonation products ($\alpha_{\text{gas}} = 0.794$) interact through non-linear interphase momentum transfer. Programmed spherical burn initiates the reaction at $r = 0$, driving a spherical detonation wave outward. Because front arrival times are kinematically enforced by the programmed burn model ($t_{\text{det}} = r/D_{\text{det}}$), verifying detonation speed is tautological. The critical physical verification instead tests multi-material shock transmission across the charge boundary, geometric spherical attenuation, two-fluid particulate drag acceleration, and particle diameter regression.
              \item \emph{Inert Multiphase Baseline and Temporal Max Tracking:} In the non-reacting baseline case (\path{ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical}), gas-phase chemical reactions are suppressed, isolating the multi-fluid hydrodynamic expansion. To ensure a strictly controlled physical baseline, both the inert case and the reacting variant use identical multi-temperature JANAF thermodynamics and Abel--Nobel real-gas equation of state for the carrier gas phase, differing solely by whether chemical reaction source terms are active (\texttt{combustionModel none} vs.\ \texttt{laminar}). To eliminate the spatial discretization artifacts and missing extrema associated with sparse discrete probes, a specialized \texttt{temporalMax} function object was developed in \texttt{libblastFunctionObjects.so}. By tracking peak field values at every time step at each cell on the coarse mesh (mapping child cells to their parent coarse cell to ensure peak preservation even across adaptive mesh refinement), \texttt{temporalMax} delivers continuous 400-point radial peak pressure attenuation curves (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_multiphase}(a)). The profile resolves the growth of the detonation peak to $12.74\,\text{GPa}$ at the charge edge, the drop across the explosive--air interface at $R_0 = 50\,\text{mm}$ (to $1.40\,\text{GPa}$), and the attenuation in air ($33\,\text{MPa}$ at $80\,\text{mm}$, $17\,\text{MPa}$ at $100\,\text{mm}$). By eliminating reliance on sparse discrete probe sampling, \texttt{temporalMax} accurately captures the global envelope of peak shock loading without spatial aliasing or under-sampling.
              \item \emph{Reacting Gas Phase with Finite-Rate Chemistry:} In the reacting variant (\path{ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_reacting}), the carrier gas phase is modelled as a reactive multicomponent mixture governed by \texttt{reactingPhaseFluid}\allowbreak\texttt{BlastThermo} with \texttt{sutherlandTransport}, \texttt{janafBlastThermo}, and the \texttt{AbelNobel} real-gas equation of state across 10 chemical species. A 4th-order adaptive Cash--Karp Runge--Kutta ODE solver (\texttt{RKCK45}) integrates finite-rate Arrhenius kinetics for methane and carbon monoxide oxidation. In this early microsecond detonation regime ($t \le 20\,\mu\text{s}$), the blast wave expands into ambient air ($N_2/O_2$) before intermediate fuel species ($CO, CH_4$) can mix and combust, which occurs on millisecond mixing timescales ($t \sim 0.1\text{--}5\,\text{ms}$). Consequently both formulations give the same detonation velocity ($7661\,\text{m/s}$), similar breakout peaks ($1.40$ vs.\ $1.60\,\text{GPa}$) and particle velocities of $1825$ vs.\ $1572\,\text{m/s}$ (where $\alpha_\text{Al} > 10^{-3}$).
              \item \emph{Particulate Aluminium Combustion via Physically Calibrated Burn-Rate Law:} In the calibrated aluminium combustion benchmark (\path{ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_alCombustion}), particulate aluminium consumption is dynamically integrated using the empirical pressure-dependent burn-rate law ($r_b(p) = a(p \cdot p_{\text{scale}})^n$ for $p \ge p_{\text{min}}$) with \texttt{diameterModel reacting;} and \texttt{reactionRate pressureBased;}. The physical coefficients ($d_0 = 30.0\,\mu\text{m}$, $\text{offset} = 0$, $p_{\text{scale}} = 10^{-6}\,\text{Pa}^{-1}$, $n = 0.7$, $a = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\text{m/s}$, and $p_{\text{min}} = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\text{Pa}$) eliminate artificial microsecond consumption and calibrate the model to experimental atomized aluminium burning in explosive detonation products ($t_{\text{burn}} \sim 0.5\text{--}1.5\,\text{ms}$). Within the charge ($r \le 50\,\text{mm}$) the peak pressure reaches $17.21\,\text{GPa}$, with $2.89\,\text{GPa}$ at breakout (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_multiphase}(a)); $d_p$ regresses from $30.00\,\mu\text{m}$ to $29.24\,\mu\text{m}$ ($\Delta d_p = 0.76\,\mu\text{m}$, $2.5\%$) by $t = 20\,\mu\text{s}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_multiphase}(d)). In contrast, unshocked ambient air ($p < p_{\text{min}}$) exhibits strictly zero regression ($d_p = 30.00\,\mu\text{m}$). The detonation front propagates at $D_{\text{det}} = 7661.3\,\text{m/s}$, within $0.64\%$ of the \texttt{EuDetCode} CJ velocity ($7612.3\,\text{m/s}$). Dynamic gas-phase heat release $\dot{Q}_{\text{gas}}$ confirms continuous active oxidation kinetics. By coupling physical particle regression with rate-limited afterburning physics, the model avoids both time-step stiffness and unphysical early thermal distortion.
          \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary and Development Roadmap}
% ============================================================

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 0 (Partially complete):} Implementation and verification of the automated test runner (\path{bin/run_blast_tests.py}), the thermodynamic reconciliation tool (\path{utilities/thermo_fitter/fit_ejwlc_nobleabel.py}) enforcing exact $C^1$ pressure continuity ($\Delta p = 0.000\%$) and reference energy offset ($E_0$), and coupling to the external \texttt{EuDetCode} ideal detonation C++ suite. Open: the tabulated BKW/exp-6 equation-of-state pipeline (WP~0.3) and per-step conservation monitoring with error norms.
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Partially complete):} 0D unit physics and reaction kinetics verification. Verified isochoric phase transfer in \texttt{blastEulerFoam} (mass, momentum and energy conserved to $\approx 5 \times 10^{-9}$; $0.59\%$ maximum pressure variation), and, in standalone single-particle models (not yet implemented in the solvers), single-particle aluminium surface heating using the Goodman Heat-Balance Integral Method (HBIM) capturing the internal thermal penetration layer and resolving early surface ignition ($16.4\%$ delay reduction vs.\ lumped models), melting latent heat buffering ($397\,\text{kJ/kg}$ at $933\,\text{K}$), oxide shell rupture at $2050\,\text{K}$, Beckstead multi-oxidizer combustion ($d^{1.9}$ law), exothermic alumina condensation ($19.7\,\text{MJ/kg}$), and high-temperature air dissociation up to $6000\,\text{K}$. Open: the isobaric transfer case, solver implementation of the aluminium particle models, and comparison with experimental ignition and burn-time data.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Largely complete):} 1D canonical multiphase numerics and cross-solver verification. Validated high-Mach shock waves in dissociating air ($\text{Ma} = 3\text{--}10$), multiphase contact discontinuity advection across Riemann solvers (\texttt{HLLC}, \texttt{AUSM+}, \texttt{Kurganov}, \texttt{Tadmor}) with oscillation suppression via the \texttt{Minmod} limiter, cross-validation against \texttt{gunIB1D} on particulate shock interaction benchmarks (Houim 1, 2, 3; the \texttt{gunIB1D} comparison could not be repeated with the current \texttt{gunIB1D} build), 1D planar CJ/ZND detonation resolving the CJ state ($P_{\text{CJ}} \approx 25\,\text{GPa}$), Von Neumann spike, and self-similar Taylor expansion fan, and dynamic coupled phase transfer in propagating detonation waves (\texttt{planar\_znd\_phaseTransfer}) with smooth conversion at $p \le 100\,\text{bar}$. Open: the compressible (Henderson / Carlson--Hoglund) drag laws of WP~2.3.
    \item \textbf{Stage 3 (In progress):} 1D spherically symmetric blast calibration against Kingery--Bulmash empirical standards (\path{tests/1D_spherical/spherical_blast}) alongside 1D spherical multiphase detonation cases in \texttt{blastEulerFoam} (\path{ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical}, \path{ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_reacting}, and \path{ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_alCombustion}). Validated incident overpressure attenuation across $Z = 0.5\text{--}3.0\,\text{m/kg}^{1/3}$ ($10\text{--}20\%$ error) and positive phase impulse ($3.41\%$ at $Z=0.5\,\text{m}$, $8.54\%$ at $Z=2.0\,\text{m}$) using a clean 300-cell 1D spherical sector simulation in \texttt{blastFoam}, alongside spherical multiphase detonation (detonation velocity within $0.64\%$, breakout $1.40\,\text{GPa}$, air shock attenuation, particle drag acceleration to $\approx 1570\text{--}1830\,\text{m/s}$), finite-rate gas-phase chemistry with matched JANAF Abel--Nobel real-gas thermodynamics, and empirical aluminium burn-rate regression ($d_0 = 30\,\mu\text{m}$, $\Delta d_p = 0.76\,\mu\text{m}$ in $20\,\mu\text{s}$). Open: peak overpressure accuracy (currently $10\text{--}20\%$ against the $5\%$ Gate~3 target, for the C4 rather than the inert Comp-B/Al baseline), the phase-transfer sensitivity study, long-time three-phase blast and afterburning runs, quantification of the reactive impulse gain, and the BKW single-EOS trial.
    \item \textbf{Stage 4 (Dispersed Multiphase Frameworks):} Comparative evaluation of Eulerian vs.\ QMOM vs.\ Dense/Dilute Lagrangian vs.\ Hybrid Handover approaches for dispersed aluminium particles, implementing high-Mach drag models (Henderson / Carlson--Hoglund) and dynamic parcel seeding.
    \item \textbf{Stage 5 (Multi-D Instabilities \& Radiation):} 2D axisymmetric charge detonation, Richtmyer--Meshkov and Rayleigh--Taylor instability-driven turbulent mixing, adaptive mesh refinement (AMR), and thermal radiation coupling via \texttt{blastRadFoam}.
    \item \textbf{Stage 6 (Final Validation \& Downselection):}\\
          Full 3D aluminized blast simulation (\texttt{ChargeCompBAl}), multi-criteria decision matrix scoring, and production modelling guidelines.
\end{itemize}
